What do EEG foundation models learn about children's brains?

Eleven foundation models compared with the EEG power spectrum in 373 children

Selin Mutlu, Duke University

Working notes, last updated November 7, 2026

CodeHBN-EEG dataLaBraM paperselinmutlu.com

I'm interested in whether EEG foundation models learn information about brain development and autism beyond the spectral features we already measure. As a first test, I predicted age from eyes-closed resting EEG in 373 children from the Healthy Brain Network. Embeddings from pretrained LaBraM reached a nested cross-validated R² of 0.59, compared with 0.49 for standard spectral features and 0.51 for the same architecture with random weights. That corresponds to a mean absolute error of 1.69 years versus 1.85 years. Pretrained LaBraM had the higher R² in 24 of 25 test folds (corrected resampled t-test, p = .013). However, when I removed the information the standard features can explain from the embeddings, pretrained and random-weights LaBraM kept similar amounts of age information (R² 0.09 and 0.12). And when I compared LaBraM with the full power spectrum (every half-hertz from 1 to 45 Hz on all 19 channels) instead of 229 summary features, the two performed the same (R² 0.60 vs. 0.59, p = .90), and LaBraM kept no significant age information once the full spectrum was removed. So with a linear readout, LaBraM's advantage over standard features comes from the standard features summarizing away spectral detail, not from information beyond the spectrum. I then ran the same comparison on ten other EEG foundation models. None of them predicted age significantly better than the full spectrum, and adding any of them to the spectrum did not significantly help. BIOT, the one model that uses a bipolar montage, kept a small amount of age information after the spectrum was removed (R² 0.08). It is not an autism result. It is a baseline for the autism question.

1. Motivation

Many studies have looked for EEG differences in autism, but the effects that replicate in large samples tend to be small and inconsistent. Most of this work tests features defined in advance, such as power in each frequency band.

Foundation models are pretrained on large amounts of unlabeled EEG, so they could in principle capture structure that predefined features miss. Their representations are also hard to interpret, and recent work (Tang et al., 2026) suggests that much of what they encode overlaps with spectral power. Before asking whether they carry autism-related information, I wanted to check whether they carry information about children's EEG that standard features do not. Age is a reasonable first target because it has a strong, well-characterized EEG signature and is also a major confound in autism EEG studies.

This page covers the first step. The next steps are to run the same comparison with autism diagnoses (section 5) and then to use sparse autoencoders to see what the relevant embedding features correspond to.

2. Data and pipeline

HBN collected 128-channel EEG from children and adolescents aged 5 to 21. I used the resting-state task from releases 1 to 3 (405 participants with resting data, 373 after quality control). Each recording went through the same steps:

  1. Interpolate noisy channels, re-reference to the average, band-pass 0.5 to 75 Hz, and notch filter at 60 Hz.
  2. Take the five 40 s eyes-closed blocks and cut them into 15 s windows at 200 Hz. Reject windows with peak-to-peak amplitude above 500 µV. Participants need at least 2 clean windows.
  3. Compute three feature sets: standard features (229 values: absolute and relative band power, aperiodic offset and exponent, alpha peak frequency), pretrained LaBraM (mean token embedding from each of its 12 layers), and LaBraM with random weights (same architecture, untrained).
  4. Fit ridge regression with nested 5-fold cross-validation, repeated 5 times. The LaBraM layer is selected within each training set, so test participants do not influence that choice.
  5. Report accuracy (R²) and a robustness score adapted from Carson et al. (2024). My version compares the predictions of two models trained on different subsets, rather than their coefficients, so it applies to embeddings.

3. Results

3.1 Pretrained LaBraM predicts age more accurately

Pretrained LaBraM explained about 59% of the variance in age, compared with about 49% for standard features. Its predictions were off by 1.69 years on average, compared with 1.85 years for standard features and 2.83 years for always predicting the mean age. It also had the smallest spread across folds and the highest robustness score.

3.2 The difference is consistent across folds

To check that the average was not driven by a few splits, I compared the feature sets fold by fold on the same test participants. Because repeated cross-validation folds overlap, the counts alone are not independent tests, so I also used the corrected resampled t-test of Nadeau & Bengio (2003), which accounts for that overlap.

3.3 The improvement depends on pretraining

The random-weights model produces embeddings of the same size as the pretrained one but performed about the same as the standard features. Every pretrained layer scored higher than every random-weights layer, and layers 2 to 4 were already close to the best, which suggests much of the age-related information is present early in the network.

3.4 LaBraM appears to contain most of the standard-feature information

To test whether LaBraM adds information beyond the standard features, or simply re-encodes them, I fit a model on both together. Combining them beat standard features alone in all 25 folds (+0.12 R², p = .017 after correction), but it did not meaningfully beat LaBraM alone (+0.02 R², 16 of 25 folds, p = .48). In other words, the standard features add little once LaBraM is included, while LaBraM adds a lot to the standard features. This fits with Tang et al. (2026), who found that foundation-model embeddings encode much of the spectrum. Nested selection most often chose layer 8 for the combined model, compared with layer 3 for LaBraM alone.

3.5 The pretraining advantage is mostly in spectral information

The combined model cannot tell whether LaBraM adds new information or just a cleaner version of the spectrum. To separate these, I removed from each embedding everything a ridge regression on the standard features could explain (fit on the training fold only) and predicted age from what was left.

Random-weights LaBraM, standard features removed0.12 (0.09)22 / 25.024

Both pretrained and random-weights embeddings kept a small amount of age information after the standard features were removed, and the two did not differ (pretrained minus random: −0.03 R², 8 of 25 folds, p = .45). So the leftover signal does not depend on pretraining. It is more likely detail that any convolutional projection of the raw signal picks up and that 229 summary features miss. Since pretrained LaBraM beats random weights by about 0.09 R² overall but not on the residuals, the pretraining advantage appears to sit in the part shared with the standard features. A reasonable reading is that pretraining makes spectral information easier to read out linearly, rather than adding new kinds of information. This is close to the "better spectral estimator" explanation I wanted to rule in or out.

The third row should be read cautiously. Ridge residualization removes only part of the explainable variance, so some shared information remains in every residual. That likely explains why standard features keep R² = 0.17 after removing LaBraM even though adding them to LaBraM barely helped (section 3.4). Section 3.6 runs a more direct test.

3.6 The full spectrum matches LaBraM

The most direct test of the "better spectral reader" explanation is to give the linear model the full power spectrum instead of 229 summaries: the log power at every half-hertz from 1 to 45 Hz on all 19 channels (1,691 values per child), from the same cleaned windows. I reprocessed all 373 children for this and used the same 25 nested folds.

Table 4. Predicting age with the full power spectrum (n = 373, 25 nested folds). p-values are from the corrected resampled t-test.

The full spectrum beat the standard features in all 25 folds (+0.09 R², p = .012) and performed the same as LaBraM (LaBraM better in 10 of 25 folds, p = .90). After removing what the full spectrum explains, LaBraM's embeddings kept almost no age information (R² 0.04, 20 of 25 folds above zero, p = .14). The reverse was not true: the full spectrum kept substantial age information after removing what LaBraM explains (R² 0.33, p = .004), though incomplete ridge residualization inflates this number, as in section 3.5. Combining the two did slightly better than either alone (0.65; vs. full spectrum p = .027, vs. LaBraM p = .083), so LaBraM may add a small amount, but most of what it carries about age is already in the spectrum.

Together with section 3.5, this answers the question I started with, at least for age and a linear readout. Pretrained LaBraM looks like a good reader of the EEG spectrum. Its advantage over the usual 229 features comes from those features discarding spectral detail, and the same advantage is available without a foundation model. This matches Tang et al. (2026). Standard features scored 0.51 in this rerun rather than 0.49, from a small difference in standard-feature extraction between computing environments; the LaBraM embeddings were identical.

3.7 The difference appeared with a larger sample

With release 1 alone (123 participants), the three feature sets performed similarly, and individual recordings had a large effect on single folds. With releases 1 to 3, pretrained LaBraM improved while the other two stayed about the same. This is a reminder that results from smaller samples should be treated cautiously, and I plan to check the result again as more releases are added.

3.8 Known developmental changes show up in the data

Two well-established developmental changes appear in this sample: alpha peak frequency increases with age and the aperiodic exponent decreases. The LaBraM embeddings also show an age gradient without being trained on age.

3.9 Attention and p-factor were not predicted

HBN also includes parent-reported CBCL scores. For the attention factor and the p-factor, all three feature sets had R² near zero with 362 participants (attention: −0.01, 0.00, and 0.00 for standard, pretrained, and random; p-factor: −0.02, −0.01, and 0.00). Neither standard features nor LaBraM embeddings predicted these scores from eyes-closed resting EEG. This suggests resting-state EEG may also be limited for autism-related traits, which is consistent with the literature below.

3.10 Movie watching did not change this

Resting state might simply be the wrong condition, so I repeated the analysis on the same children's EEG while they watched two short films, The Present (400 usable recordings) and Despicable Me (402). Besides the same spectral and LaBraM features, I measured neural synchrony: for each child and electrode, the correlation between their 1 to 20 Hz signal (and their alpha and theta power over time) and the average of all other children at the same moment of the film.

Movie EEG predicted age about as well as rest, and synchrony carried age information on its own (R² 0.25, and 0.19 in Despicable Me), consistent with Petroni et al. (2018), who found that synchrony to these films declines with age in HBN. Adding synchrony to the movie spectrum did not improve age prediction (p = .27). None of the feature sets, at rest or during either film, predicted the four CBCL scores. The one nominally significant value (rest LaBraM for externalizing, uncorrected p = .02) does not survive correction across the roughly 40 tests here. Mean synchrony was also unrelated to the CBCL scores after controlling for age and data quality (all |ρ| < 0.09). My synchrony measure is simpler than the correlated component analysis used by Petroni et al., so a stronger method could still find something, but parent-reported dimensions do not seem to be reachable from these EEG features in either condition.

3.11 Ten other foundation models

LaBraM is one model, so I repeated the comparison with ten others available through Braindecode and Hugging Face: BIOT, ST-EEGFormer (small), EEGPT, REVE (base), CBraMod, EEGDINO (small), LUNA (base), SignalJEPA, CodeBrain, and BENDR. Each model received the same four 15 s eyes-closed windows per child, prepared the way its own paper describes (channel order, sampling rate, segment length, and amplitude scaling). I took the mean-pooled output of each transformer block, selected the block inside the training folds as before, and ran the same 25 nested folds. The full spectrum was recomputed from the same four windows, and every model was also run with random weights.

No model predicted age significantly better than the full spectrum, and no combination of a model with the spectrum significantly beat the spectrum alone (smallest corrected p = .056, for BIOT). Eight models came within 0.07 R² of the spectrum in either direction, and three performed much worse. For EEGPT, REVE, LaBraM, and ST-EEGFormer, pretraining mattered a great deal (pretrained minus random +0.25 to +0.53 R², all corrected p < .001), but it brought them up to the spectrum rather than past it. For LUNA, EEGDINO, and CBraMod, the random-weights version already came within 0.10 R² of the pretrained one, so their architecture alone produces a representation close to the spectrum.

The one exception is BIOT, which kept a small but significant amount of age information after the full spectrum was removed (R² 0.08, corrected p = .031). BIOT is also the only model here that takes a bipolar montage (differences between neighboring electrodes) and normalizes each segment's amplitude, so its input carries relationships between channels that a per-channel spectrum does not. This is a hypothesis, not yet a result. A direct check would be to add the spectrum of the same bipolar channels, and coherence between neighbors, to the baseline. BENDR's small residual (R² 0.02, p = .047) is not meaningful given its near-zero overall performance.

The three weak models should be read cautiously. BENDR was no better than its random-weights version, which points to a mismatch between my preprocessing and what it expects rather than a property of the model. SignalJEPA and CodeBrain may also suffer from differences between their pretraining setup and 19-channel pediatric data. These results also use a linear readout of frozen embeddings, which recent work suggests understates what fine-tuning can reach (Yang et al., 2026).

4. Prior work on autism EEG markers

In a meta-analysis of 41 resting-state studies (Neo et al., 2023), relative alpha (lower in autism) and gamma (higher) showed the clearest pooled effects. Large samples have been less encouraging: LEAP (Garcés et al., 2022, n = 411) found no group differences after correction, and ABC-CT (Webb et al., 2023) found the face-evoked N170 to be a stronger candidate than resting measures. Carson et al. (2024) showed that selecting models for reproducibility gives more stable associations, which is why robustness is reported alongside accuracy here.

5. Next steps

For the autism question, I'm planning to use movie-watching EEG rather than resting state. HBN recorded the same participants' EEG while they watched short films, including The Present and Despicable Me. In HBN fMRI, autistic children showed lower synchrony with peers in the theory-of-mind network during a movie (Lyons et al., 2020, d = 0.93), which suggests naturalistic social stimuli may reveal differences that rest does not. Movie EEG did not help with the CBCL scores (section 3.10), but a clinical diagnosis is a different target from a parent questionnaire, so the movie recordings remain the better place to test autism.

  1. Strengthen the baseline further. The full-spectrum comparison is done (section 3.6). Ten more foundation models are done (section 3.11). Next are connectivity and the other feature families from Tang et al. (2026), including the bipolar spectrum and coherence that could explain BIOT's residual, and a nonlinear readout.
  2. Improve the synchrony measure. The first pass is done (section 3.10). Next are correlated component analysis, the standard method in this literature, and synchrony restricted to the film's social scenes, where the fMRI difference was found.
  3. Obtain diagnostic labels. HBN's clinician diagnoses, IQ, and trait measures require a data use agreement signed by a faculty sponsor.
  4. Test autism with the same procedure. Nested cross-validation, identical held-out participants for every feature set, age, sex, IQ, site, and epilepsy as covariates, and robustness reported alongside accuracy.

I would appreciate input on two questions: whether the movie-watching direction fits what COMET has seen with stimulus-driven EEG, and whether there is a path to the HBN data agreement or to testing results in a preschool cohort.

6. Limitations

  • Age is not autism. These results show that pretrained LaBraM represents developmental information in EEG about as well as the full power spectrum, and better than 229 summary features. They do not address autism.
  • Linear readout only. All comparisons use ridge regression. A nonlinear readout could find information in the embeddings that a linear model cannot, and connectivity, complexity, and cross-frequency measures have not been tested yet.
  • Residualization is linear and incomplete. Removing standard-feature information with ridge regression leaves some shared variance and ignores nonlinear relationships, so the residual results (section 3.5) are approximate.
  • The statistical evidence is modest. The main comparisons pass a corrected resampled t-test (p = .047 after Holm correction), but all results come from one dataset. Testing the fitted models on a held-out HBN release would be a stronger check.
  • EEG brain age is well studied. A foundation model pretrained on HBN itself reported higher accuracy over a wider age range (STST-JEPA, 2026). The contribution here is the controlled comparison against standard features and random weights.
  • Frozen embeddings, few conditions. Eleven models were compared without fine-tuning, on eyes-closed rest (LaBraM also on two films), with 19 channels and embeddings averaged across tokens. Preprocessing for some models, BENDR in particular, may not match their pretraining.
  • Site and data quality are not yet modeled. HBN spans several sites, and 32 of 405 participants were excluded for too few clean windows.
